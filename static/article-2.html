Iran Threatens Hormuz Blockade as Gulf Tanker Attack Propels Oil Over $105

✍️ By Abhishek Pandey, WorldWire Desk  |  ⏱️ Published October 08, 2026 at 15:19 UTC  |  📰 Wire Attribution: Reuters
Photojournalism wire visual • Attributed to Reuters editorial archive
Executive Briefing: Maritime security in the Persian Gulf deteriorated sharply after a commercial tanker was struck by multiple projectiles off Qatar and Tehran warned regional nations against establishing alternative oil corridors. The escalation has reduced vessel transits through the Strait of Hormuz to multi-month lows, propelling global crude prices above $105 a barrel.

Commercial shipping across the Persian Gulf faced severe disruption on Thursday after an oil tanker sailing north of Qatar was struck by multiple projectiles, inflicting casualties and intensifying an already volatile maritime crisis. According to maritime monitoring data from Kpler, vessel transits through the strategic Strait of Hormuz dropped to their lowest levels in more than two months following a surge in attacks. In response, global energy benchmarks spiked, with Brent crude surging roughly 5% to breach $105.20 per barrel as market fears over sustained supply chokepoints took hold.

Tehran issued fresh warnings on Thursday declaring that any attempts by Gulf neighbors to establish unauthorized rerouting mechanisms or alternative export channels would be regarded as hostile actions. Iranian Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi stated that diplomatic consultations over a framework to reopen the corridor remain active, noting that Tehran is reviewing Washington's latest response. However, regional diplomats and security officials confirmed that the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC) has deployed intimidation maneuvers—including missile and drone capabilities—to ensure Iran retains strategic leverage over the strait. Meanwhile, producers such as Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates continue attempts to redirect volumes through overland pipelines like the East-West conduit.

With nearly a fifth of the world's petroleum normally flowing through the Strait of Hormuz, the escalating conflict threatens acute inflationary shocks across Europe, North America, and import-dependent economies in the Asia-Pacific. Major liquefied natural gas (LNG) export deliveries remain constrained under extended force majeure declarations, forcing key Asian buyers to seek emergency credit and fuel arrangements. Unless diplomatic channels yield an immediate de-escalation framework, international maritime bodies warn that further military intercepts could trigger direct naval clashes and an enduring energy supply crunch.
