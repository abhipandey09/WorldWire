US Freezes Green Card Sponsorship for Microsoft and Major Tech Firms in Visa Crackdown

✍️ By Abhishek Pandey, WorldWire Desk  |  ⏱️ Published October 10, 2026 at 10:53 UTC  |  📰 Wire Attribution: Bloomberg
Photojournalism wire visual • Attributed to Bloomberg editorial archive
Executive Briefing: The Trump administration has indefinitely suspended Microsoft, Adobe, and six leading IT multinational corporations from the Permanent Labor Certification (PERM) program, blocking them from sponsoring skilled foreign employees for permanent U.S. residency. Vice President JD Vance and Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling accused the tech giants of systemic visa abuse and displacing American workers, marking the most aggressive federal intervention against Silicon Valley's foreign talent pipeline in decades.

In a seismic escalation of corporate immigration enforcement, the White House announced an indefinite freeze on green card labor certifications for Microsoft Corp., Adobe Inc., and major information technology contractors including Infosys, Tata Consultancy Services, Wipro, Cognizant, HCL Technologies, and Capgemini. Under directives issued by the U.S. Department of Labor, federal authorities have halted the intake of new Permanent Labor Certification (PERM) petitions and frozen all pending applications associated with the eight corporate entities. Secretary of Labor Keith Sonderling confirmed that the sanctions stem from multiple active federal investigations into alleged labor certification fraud, asserting that the targeted firms collectively accounted for hundreds of thousands of foreign worker filings while domestic technology payrolls experienced widespread cutbacks.

Speaking at a White House press briefing, Vice President JD Vance sharply rebuked Silicon Valley's corporate labor practices, singling out Microsoft for what he termed an exploitation of high-skilled immigration pathways. Vance alleged that the software giant shed thousands of domestic tech workers while maintaining heavy reliance on temporary visa holders, arguing that corporations must prioritize domestic recruitment rather than substituting homegrown engineering talent with lower-cost foreign staff. In response, Microsoft forcefully defended its employment standards, stating that the overwhelming majority of its domestic workforce is American, that H-1B visa holders receive top-tier market compensation comparable to their domestic peers, and that the vast majority of its recent filings were routine status extensions for established staff.

The clampdown has sent shockwaves through the global technology sector and sparked immediate diplomatic friction, with foreign industry associations warning that dismantling the PERM pathway imperils international talent retention and enterprise software consulting networks across North America and South Asia. Beyond the corporate suspensions, the administration revealed companion federal probes into nine prestigious research universities over international scholar programs. As legal challenges and corporate appeals prepare to hit federal courts, multinational technology firms face an abrupt staffing crisis that could compel widespread organizational restructuring and accelerate offshore tech operations.
