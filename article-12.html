CHIME Telescope Detects 9-Billion-Year-Old Hydrogen Glow to Probe Dark Energy

✍️ By Abhishek Pandey, WorldWire Desk  |  ⏱️ Published October 10, 2026 at 10:49 UTC  |  📰 Wire Attribution: ScienceDaily
Photojournalism wire visual • Attributed to ScienceDaily editorial archive
Executive Briefing: In a major cosmological milestone, the CHIME radio telescope collaboration has achieved the first standalone detection of ancient neutral hydrogen emissions dating back nine billion years. Published in The Astrophysical Journal, the breakthrough confirms that hydrogen intensity mapping works independently without relying on optical galaxy surveys, unlocking an unprecedented method to investigate dark energy.

In a landmark achievement for observational cosmology, researchers operating the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME) announced the first standalone detection of the faint 21-centimeter radio glow emitted by neutral atomic hydrogen gas from when the universe was less than half its current age. Published in The Astrophysical Journal, the study evaluated observations across the 608.2 to 707.8 megahertz radio band, capturing the signature of ancient cosmic gas at redshifts between z = 1.01 and 1.34. The international collaboration detected the clustered hydrogen signal at an extraordinary statistical significance of 12.4 sigma—far eclipsing the standard 5-sigma gold standard for verified scientific discovery—revealing that roughly two percent of the cosmos's hydrogen existed in neutral atomic form nine billion years ago.

Until now, cosmologists attempting to isolate ancient hydrogen signatures had to cross-correlate faint radio data with existing optical galaxy catalogs produced by other observatories to filter out overwhelming galactic and terrestrial radio interference. By developing advanced signal-processing and foreground-removal pipelines, the joint research team—comprising astronomers from the University of British Columbia, McGill University, the University of Toronto, and India's Raman Research Institute—successfully isolated the cosmic signal utilizing CHIME's internal data alone. "Hydrogen is the most common element in the universe and the raw material from which stars form," explained co-author Dr. Arnab Chakraborty of the University of Toronto. "Its faint radio emission acts like a cosmic tracer, revealing how matter is distributed across space and allowing researchers to test competing ideas about the expansion of the cosmos."

The demonstration delivers a validated, cost-effective framework for mapping vast expanses of the observable universe to resolve the nature of dark energy, the mysterious force accelerating cosmic expansion that comprises approximately 68 percent of the universe. By removing the costly requirement of optical galaxy mapping, astronomers can now conduct three-dimensional surveys of cosmic structure far faster and over deeper cosmic lookback times. The success also establishes critical operational foundations for next-generation international megascience facilities, including the Square Kilometre Array (SKA), setting the stage for decisive tests of cosmic evolution in the coming decade.
